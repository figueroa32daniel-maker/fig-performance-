What vibration therapy is

Whole-body vibration therapy means standing, sitting or holding a position on a platform that vibrates quickly. The vibration makes your muscles contract and relax many times a second. You can stand still on it, or do simple moves like a squat or a calf raise while it runs.

Does vibration therapy actually work?

It can help a little, but it isn't magic. According to the Mayo Clinic, some research suggests whole-body vibration may help muscle strength, balance in older adults, back pain and bone loss, and may support weight loss when paired with eating less. The research is limited, though, and it's not clear that it's as good for you as regular exercise.

So we're straight with you: a vibration plate won't build muscle or burn fat on its own. Coached strength training, good nutrition and daily walking do that. Vibration therapy is one part of recovery, not a shortcut.

What is 10 minutes on a vibration plate equivalent to?

There's no reliable answer. Claims like "10 minutes equals an hour at the gym" come from marketing, not research. Ten minutes on a plate is ten minutes of light muscle work. Treat it as a warm-up or a recovery tool, and keep your real training.

Who should not do vibration therapy?

Skip it, or get your doctor's OK first, if any of these apply to you:

  • You're pregnant.
  • You have a pacemaker, defibrillator or other implanted electronic device.
  • You have a heart condition, an arrhythmia or uncontrolled high blood pressure.
  • You've had recent surgery, especially a joint replacement or spine surgery.
  • You have a blood clot, deep vein thrombosis or a clotting disorder.

If you're not sure, ask your physician. Vibration therapy is offered for general wellness. It doesn't diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease. See our health disclaimer.

How we use it at Fig Performance

  • Most members use it for a few minutes after training, often with red light therapy on the same visit.
  • Stop if anything feels wrong: dizziness, pain, or numbness.

Straight answers.

Does vibration therapy actually work?

Some research suggests whole-body vibration may help muscle strength, balance in older adults and bone loss, but the research is limited and it isn't as proven as regular exercise. At Fig Performance we use it as part of recovery, not as a replacement for strength training.

Who should not do vibration therapy?

People who are pregnant, have a pacemaker or other implanted device, have a heart condition or uncontrolled high blood pressure, have had recent surgery such as a joint replacement, or have a blood clot or clotting disorder should skip it or get their doctor's OK first.

What is 10 minutes on a vibration plate equivalent to?

There's no reliable equivalent. Claims that 10 minutes on a plate equals a full workout come from marketing, not research. Use it as a warm-up or recovery tool alongside real training.

Can vibration therapy help you lose weight?

Not on its own. Some studies show it may support weight loss when combined with eating fewer calories, but fat loss after 40 comes from strength training, nutrition and daily activity.
